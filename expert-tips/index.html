The homeowner’s guide

Start with what you’re noticing. These notes can help you describe the problem and choose a sensible next step.

01

Read the signs

One slow drain or a whole-house problem?

Where the symptoms show up can help narrow down where to look first.

A single slow sink or tub often points to a blockage close to that fixture. If several fixtures are slow or backing up around the same time, the restriction may be farther along a shared drain or sewer line.

Make a quick note

  • Which fixtures are affected, and which still drain normally?
  • Does water back up in another fixture when a toilet flushes or a sink runs?
  • Did the issue start suddenly, or has it been getting worse over time?
If wastewater is coming up through a floor drain or another fixture, stop running water and treat it as a possible sewer backup.

02

Catch it early

Gurgling, odors and recurring slow drains

A drain can give you clues before it stops moving altogether.

Listen for repeated bubbling or gurgling, notice whether a sewer-like odor returns, and pay attention to drains that keep slowing down after they have been cleared. One symptom by itself doesn’t identify the cause, but a pattern is useful information.

Don’t mask a recurring symptom

Fragrance, repeated plunging or a quick chemical treatment may hide the warning without explaining why it came back. If the same fixture clogs again, or multiple fixtures begin acting up, note the timing and ask for the line to be assessed.

A persistent sewage odor or any wastewater backup deserves prompt attention. Keep children and pets away from affected areas.
03

Daily habits

What should—and should not—go down a drain

Small habits can reduce avoidable buildup in household plumbing.

Use a sink strainer to catch food scraps, and a hair catcher in showers and tubs. Let cooking grease cool, then put it in the trash instead of rinsing it into the sink. Wipe greasy pans before washing them.

Keep the toilet simple

Flush human waste and toilet paper only. “Flushable” wipes, paper towels, cotton swabs, dental floss and hygiene products can snag or accumulate in plumbing even when a package says they can be flushed.

If your home uses a septic system, be especially careful about grease, wipes and household chemicals; follow your septic provider’s guidance as well. The EPA advises septic owners to avoid chemical drain openers and to keep grease and non-flushable items out of the system.

04

Before a quick fix

Think twice before using a chemical drain opener

A strong product can make a later service visit more hazardous.

Read product labels carefully and never mix drain products with other cleaners. If a chemical opener has already been used, tell the service professional what was used and when. That information helps them plan a safer next step.

For a minor fixture clog, removing visible hair from a stopper or using an appropriate plunger may be a reasonable first step. Stop if the water level rises, several drains are affected, you suspect a sewer backup, or the clog returns quickly. Avoid forcing a tool into a drain if you don’t know what it may contact.

If you used a chemical product, don’t add another cleaner to it. Follow the product’s label directions and let the service professional know before work begins.
05

Repeated clogs

Why the same drain keeps backing up

Clearing a blockage can restore flow without explaining why it formed.

Hair, soap residue, grease and other debris can collect again. A recurring clog can also mean the restriction is deeper in the line or that the pipe has a condition that keeps catching material. The symptoms alone can’t confirm which explanation applies.

Track the pattern

Write down how soon the clog returns, whether other fixtures are involved, and whether the drain has been cleared before. That history helps distinguish a one-time fixture blockage from a recurring line issue.

When a clog returns soon after service or affects several fixtures, ask whether inspection of the accessible line would help identify the visible cause before deciding what to do next.

06

See the line

What a drain camera can tell you

A camera can add useful visual information when symptoms keep returning.

A drain camera travels through an accessible line and shows visible conditions along the portion it can reach. Depending on access and line conditions, the view may help locate buildup, an obstruction or an area that needs further attention.

Know the limits

A camera view is limited by the route, access point, water, debris and the condition of the line. It is not a guarantee that every part of a plumbing system was viewed, and a video alone may not answer every repair question. Ask what was visible, how much of the line was inspected, and what the next step would establish.

07

Sewer line clues

Can tree roots get into a sewer line?

Roots can take advantage of openings in a buried pipe, but symptoms alone can’t confirm root intrusion.

Small openings at joints or damaged sections can let moisture escape and attract nearby roots. If roots enter the line, they may catch debris and contribute to repeated blockages. A drain clearing can restore flow, but it does not repair a damaged pipe or prevent roots from returning.

Look for a pattern, not one clue

Recurring clogs, several slow fixtures or a backup can justify a closer look, especially if the problem keeps returning after clearing. A camera inspection may show visible conditions in the accessible section of line and help guide the next conversation.

Don’t dig or apply root-killing chemicals based only on a hunch. Confirm what is happening and what work is actually within scope before making a repair decision.

08

Safety first

What to do during a sewer backup

Protect people first, then limit additional water entering the system.
  1. Stop using toilets, sinks, showers, laundry and dishwashers connected to the affected drains.
  2. Keep people and pets away from sewage or wastewater. Avoid direct contact.
  3. If water is near electrical equipment, do not enter or touch equipment in the affected area.
  4. Call a qualified plumbing or drain professional and describe where the backup is appearing.

Sewage can contain germs that make people sick. Cleanup after a significant backup may require protective equipment and careful disinfection; don’t begin cleanup if you cannot do it safely. CDC guidance recommends protective gloves, boots and eye protection when handling sewage.

If sewage has entered living space, keep children and pets out and get professional guidance on safe cleanup. For immediate danger, contact local emergency services.

09

Get ready

Useful details to share before a service visit

A few observations help make the first conversation more productive.
  • The affected fixtures and whether more than one is involved.
  • When the problem started and whether it is constant or intermittent.
  • Any gurgling, odor or wastewater backup you have noticed.
  • Any previous clearing attempts or drain products already used.
  • Whether you have a septic system, if you know.

Don’t enter a crawlspace, lift a heavy cover or approach standing wastewater to gather details. A short description from a safe place is enough to get the conversation started.
